A recurring business process becomes difficult to manage when the next action exists only in somebody's memory. A customer enquiry arrives, someone forwards it, another colleague prepares a draft, and nobody knows who should approve the final response. The problem is not necessarily a lack of software. Often the first improvement is simply **making the sequence visible**.

This guide walks through a four-stage example you can sketch on paper before deciding whether any software is needed.

## 1. Pick a process that repeats

Choose one activity that occurs often enough for a clear sequence to save confusion. Customer enquiries, invoice review, staff onboarding and stock replenishment are all common categories. Avoid mapping your whole business in one sitting. Begin with one process, one typical request, and a clear start and finish.

For an example, imagine a small creative team receiving requests for work. The trigger is an incoming project brief; the desired finish is an approved hand-off to the person who will begin delivery. This is an illustrative process, not a claim about the PROWORKS organisation.

## 2. Write the stages in ordinary language

A useful first process map might have **Intake → Prepare → Human review → Hand-off**. Intake records what was requested and the right person to ask. Prepare gathers enough detail to make a responsible recommendation. Human review verifies scope, timing and quality. Hand-off tells the next owner exactly what has been agreed.

If a stage requires a sentence of explanation to understand, label it with a verb and a result. For example, replace "processing" with "Confirm missing details". Every label should answer "what changes here?" rather than merely name a department.

## 3. Give every stage an owner and an exit condition

A process map without responsibility may look neat while still leaving work stuck. For each stage, record a responsible role, not necessarily a specific person's private contact details. Add a concrete condition that permits the next step.

| Stage        | Owner role          | Ready to advance when…                                |
| ------------ | ------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Intake       | Enquiry coordinator | Request and contact pathway are understood            |
| Prepare      | Project lead        | Scope and major constraints are documented            |
| Human review | Approver            | An authorised person accepts the proposed next action |
| Hand-off     | Delivery owner      | The accepted decision and next milestone are visible  |

A small team may use the same person in multiple roles. The purpose is to make responsibility visible, not to enforce needless bureaucracy.

## 4. Design the exception paths

Most delays occur outside the ideal route. What if a requester has not provided sufficient details? What if the approver is unavailable? What if the scope changes? Draw a return arrow for missing information and a clear waiting state for unanswered decisions.

Do not silently mark an unreviewed item "approved" to keep a status chart green. A human approval gate is useful precisely because it can stop an action that is not ready. A basic paper checklist works; if the process becomes complex, a digital interface can show the same states more clearly.

## 5. Keep only useful fields

Record the request summary, current stage, owner role, next action, and review date when appropriate. If no decision depends on a field, consider removing it. More captured data does not automatically improve coordination, and unnecessary personal information creates an avoidable privacy burden.

You should also define what happens when a request is cancelled, completed or reopened. A process is easier to trust when it has a clear history that explains who changed an item and why.

## 6. Review the process with the actual team

Ask someone who performs the work to walk through a normal request and a difficult one. Test whether the stages match real behaviour, whether anyone needs a missing document, and whether approval is genuinely required. Update the map after seeing where people hesitate. Avoid assuming a visual flowchart is correct simply because it looks polished.

Once a process is understandable, consider what software would genuinely improve it. In a small business, that may mean a shared checklist. In a larger workflow, it may mean searchable documentation, task ownership and readable reporting. The right answer depends on the process and its consequences.
